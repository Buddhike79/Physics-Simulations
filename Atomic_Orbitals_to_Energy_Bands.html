Atomic energy levels

Each energy level is a horizontal line; vertical position represents energy. Positions are qualitative, not a precision atomic-structure calculation.

Why energy changes

Changing nuclear charge and shielding changes orbital energies. Electrons fill the lowest available states, subject to Pauli exclusion and Hund's rule.

From separate atomic levels to bands in a solid

Bring atoms closer together. Their electron wavefunctions overlap, and each atomic state splits into many closely spaced states. With an enormous number of atoms, these states form energy bands.

Suggested classroom sequence

1. Start with one atom

Select hydrogen, then silicon. Ask: what changed when the number of protons increased? Discuss nuclear attraction and electron shielding.

2. Count orbitals, not just shells

1s, 2s and 3s each contain one orbital (maximum 2 electrons). Each p subshell has three available orbitals (maximum 6 electrons), but only orbitals occupied by electrons are shown in the cloud view. Hund’s rule fills them singly before pairing.

3. Use Hund's rule too

For equal-energy p orbitals, electrons occupy separate orbitals with parallel spins before pairing. This explains the filling pattern in 2p and 3p.

4. Scale up to a solid

Increase N and reduce separation. Explain that one atomic level produces many allowed states. The collection of states forms bands, with a forbidden energy gap possible between valence and conduction bands.

Important distinction: Pauli exclusion is a rule for allowed electron states; it does not by itself create the band gap. Band formation comes from many atoms interacting in a periodic solid, and the gap depends on the material's electronic structure.
